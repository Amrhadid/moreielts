The quiet economics of lending libraries

AThe first public lending libraries in Britain were funded not by the state but by subscription. Members paid an annual fee, and in return could borrow from a collection chosen by a committee of their peers. By 1850 there were more than four hundred such societies, most of them in towns with fewer than ten thousand residents.

BWhat the subscription model lacked in reach it made up for in loyalty. Because members chose the stock, the collections reflected local taste with an accuracy that later municipal libraries, answerable to committees of councillors rather than readers, rarely matched. Borrowing records from Kendal show the same novels circulating for thirty years.

CThe Public Libraries Act of 1850 is usually told as the moment the state took over. The surviving accounts suggest something slower. Rate-funded libraries were permitted, not required, and a town had to vote for one. Many did not. Norwich rejected a library three times before 1857, each time on the grounds that the subscription society already served those who wished to read.

DWhere the two systems coexisted, they tended to specialise. The municipal library took reference works, newspapers and the kind of improving literature a councillor could defend in public. The subscription library kept the novels. Catalogues from Leeds in the 1870s list more than two thousand works of fiction against fewer than three hundred held by the free library a street away.

EThe subscription societies did not so much lose as dissolve. As municipal collections grew, and especially after fiction became respectable in the 1890s, the annual fee bought less and less that could not be had for nothing. By 1914 fewer than fifty remained, most of them in towns that had voted against a rate.

FIt is tempting to read this as a story about money. It is at least as much a story about who decides what a town should read, and the answer the Victorians arrived at, after sixty years of argument, was: not the readers.
